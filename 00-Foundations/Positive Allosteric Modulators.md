# Positive Allosteric Modulators

Positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) are the gold standard for real-world nootropic architectures, and the reason is a single property: **they do not fire the receptor artificially.** A direct agonist slams the receptor open regardless of whether the natural transmitter is present, which is exactly what triggers homeostatic down-regulation. A PAM instead binds to a non-orthosteric site and *amplifies* the effect of the natural transmitter when it arrives. The result is that the network keeps its natural pulsatile signaling rhythm while its structural response is significantly amplified. That is why PAMs are the preferred tool for both reinforcing and expanding a network without crashing it.

## Class Labels

Every agent in this book belongs to one of the functional classes below. The schedules in every chapter and in [[Stack Schedules]] tag each row with the code in parentheses — for example `L-Glycine (A1)` or `Huperzine A (BI)` — so that at a glance you can see what an agent is *doing* to the receptor, not just what it is. The class is a pharmacological role, not a marketing category: the same molecule can play different roles in different systems.

| Code | Class | What it does at the receptor | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A1** | Direct agonist | Binds the orthosteric site and fires the receptor directly, whether or not the natural transmitter is present. The strongest and most down-regulation-prone class. | L-Glycine, D-serine, Mucuna (L-DOPA), caffeine (adenosine A1/A2) |
| **A2** | Partial agonist | Binds the orthosteric site but opens the receptor less than the natural transmitter; acts as an agonist when the system is quiet and as a functional antagonist when it is over-active. | Rhodiola (NE), Kanna (5-HT), some phytocannabinoids |
| **A3** | Atypical / indirect agonist | Raises the natural transmitter's availability or release without binding the receptor's orthosteric site (e.g. a reuptake blocker or a release enhancer). | Kanna (SERT), Rhodiola (NE release), Mucuna (L-DOPA load) |
| **P1** | Positive allosteric modulator (PAM) | Binds a non-orthosteric site and amplifies the receptor's response to the natural transmitter. Does not fire the receptor on its own — the class this book prefers. | Piracetam (AMPA), Magnesium L-Threonate (NMDA), Galantamine (nicotinic), Honokiol ($GABA_A$) |
| **P2** | Negative allosteric modulator (NAM) | Binds a non-orthosteric site and dampens the receptor's response to the natural transmitter. Used in Expansion protocols as the intermittent antagonist. | Memantine (NMDA, prescription), Ginkgo bilobalide ($GABA_A$), scopolamine (muscarinic, prescription) |
| **BI** | Breakdown inhibitor | Inhibits the enzyme that degrades the transmitter (AChE, MAO-B, FAAH, GABA-T), lengthening the signal without firing the receptor. | Huperzine A (AChE), Selegiline (MAO-B), CBD (FAAH), Lemon Balm (GABA-T) |
| **RI** | Reuptake inhibitor | Blocks the transporter that clears the transmitter from the cleft, prolonging its presence. | Kanna (SERT), caffeine (adenosine), CBD (SERT, weak) |
| **PR** | Precursor | A raw material (amino acid, choline, fatty acid) that raises the intracellular pool of the transmitter before release. | L-Tyrosine, 5-HTP, Alpha-GPC, Arachidonic Acid |
| **CO** | Cofactor / enzyme support | A vitamin or mineral required for the synthesis or degradation machinery to work. | B6/B9/B12 (monoamine synthesis), Magnesium (NMDA gate) |
| **NA** | Neurotrophic / growth | Drives synaptogenesis, dendritic spine growth, and neurogenesis — the "fertilizer" phase of Expansion. | Lion's Mane (BDNF/NGF), UMP (membrane phospholipids), DHA (membrane) |
| **BE** | Behavioral trigger | A non-chemical intervention that drives the system's natural release (exercise, meditation, sleep, cold exposure). | Aerobic exercise (endorphins), meditation (GABA/NE), sleep (glymphatic clearance) |

The reinforce sequence in [[Network Modification Paradigms]] is therefore **PR → P1/A3 → BI/RI**, and the expand sequence is **P2 (intermittent) → NA → A1/PR (dense influx)**. The class labels in every schedule in this book encode exactly that.

## Practical Notes

1. **The PAM is almost always the *second* thing you take in a reinforce stack** — it comes after the precursor has loaded the intracellular pool, per the half-life rule.
2. **Several of the framework's "PAMs" are actually research compounds** (SKF-83959, DETQ, Sabcomeline, Neboglamine) that are not available OTC; the substitutes in the tables are the closest legal, purchasable analogs, and they are weaker, so the schedules in each chapter compensate with longer cycles rather than higher doses.
3. **Not all agents in the tables are true PAMs.** Some are precursors (L-Tyrosine, 5-HTP, L-Tryptophan, Arachidonic Acid), some are agonists (L-Glycine, D-serine), and some are indirect modulators (adaptogens, choline sources). The tables include all of them because they all contribute to the same goal: enhancing the natural signaling of the target receptor without artificially firing it.
4. **The OTC column is a guide, not a guarantee.** Availability varies by country and regulatory environment. Always check local regulations before purchasing.
5. **The class labels (A1, A2, A3, P1, P2, BI, RI, PR, CO, NA, BE) are the vocabulary of the schedules.** Every row in every stack schedule in this book carries the class code in parentheses after the agent name, so you can see at a glance what the agent is doing to the receptor. The reinforce sequence is PR → P1/A3 → BI/RI; the expand sequence is P2 (intermittent) → NA → A1/PR (dense influx).
